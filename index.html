How to read this figure

What the MI means. From the LFP power of a single trial, in one area and frequency, how well can you tell whether that trial was an oddball? 0 bits: not at all, the two kinds of trial have the same distribution of power. 1 bit, the maximum here (equal numbers of each): perfectly, the distributions do not overlap. For scale: 0.04 bits ≈ d′ 0.5 (≈60 % of single trials classified correctly), 0.16 bits ≈ d′ 1 (≈69 %), 0.5 bits ≈ d′ 2 (≈84 %).

Biologically, it measures how reliably, trial after trial, the synchronized synaptic activity that generates the LFP in that band changes when the expectation is violated. A large average change that varies a lot from trial to trial carries little information; a modest but consistent one carries more. The single-trial panel (top right) shows the two distributions whose separation the MI measures.

Direction. MI has no sign. The second heatmap row, Δ power, shows whether the information comes from more power (red) or less power (blue) on oddball trials. Dots: MI significant across animals (FDR q < 0.05).

Vs matched control. Oddball vs standard mixes a different stimulus (other orientation-tuned neurons respond) with release from adaptation (the standard has been repeated many times). The control block shows the same stimuli in random order with the same timing, so oddball vs control keeps the stimulus, and its tuning, fixed and removes the adaptation advantage. The extra column, Standard (adaptation), compares the standard with the same grating in the control block. Available for the standard and sequence paradigms.
